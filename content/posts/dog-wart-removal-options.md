Most dog warts don't need to be removed at all. That's the starting point, and skipping it leads a lot of owners down an unnecessary road of worry, expense, and sometimes home remedies that cause more harm than the wart itself.

Canine warts are caused by canine papillomavirus (CPV), and the great majority of them resolve on their own within one to five months as the dog's immune system catches up. Young dogs and immunocompromised dogs are most susceptible. The growths typically look like small, cauliflower-textured bumps, most commonly around the mouth, lips, and eyes, though they can appear anywhere. Single, smooth wart-like growths in older dogs are more often sebaceous cysts or papillomas with a different cause, so getting a confirmed diagnosis before deciding on a removal approach actually matters here.

## When to Actually Worry

Most warts don't need your intervention. But a few situations do. If a wart is bleeding repeatedly after minor contact, growing quickly (more than doubling in size over a few weeks), or is in a location that makes eating, swallowing, or seeing difficult, don't wait for spontaneous resolution. Same goes for warts on a dog who is on long-term immunosuppressive medication, like cyclosporine or corticosteroids. Those dogs may not clear the virus without help.

Also pay attention if you have multiple dogs. Canine papillomavirus is contagious between dogs, particularly at dog parks, daycares, and groomers. Isolating a dog with active warts from other dogs is a reasonable precaution, particularly for puppies.

One scenario that genuinely requires a vet the same day: a wart in or around the eye that is causing squinting, discharge, or visible irritation. The eye is not a place for watchful waiting.

## What Vets Actually Do for Removal

When a wart needs to come off, here are the real options a veterinarian might recommend:

**Surgical excision** is the most definitive method. The vet removes the growth under local or general anesthesia. It's fast, clean, and allows the tissue to be sent to a pathology lab if the diagnosis is uncertain. This is the right call for single large warts, warts that keep bleeding, or any growth where you need to know what it actually is.

**Cryotherapy (freezing)** uses liquid nitrogen to destroy wart tissue. It works well on small, accessible warts and usually doesn't require general anesthesia. Some growths need two or three treatments to fully resolve. The treated area scabs and falls off over a week or two.

**Laser ablation** is available at specialty and larger general practices. It's precise and minimizes bleeding. Useful for warts near delicate structures like the eye or in the oral cavity. It tends to cost more than cryotherapy.

**Immunostimulation** is an interesting option some vets use: crushing a small piece of the wart tissue and injecting it elsewhere in the dog, essentially creating a crude autogenous "vaccine." There's a reasonable evidence base for this in veterinary literature, though it's not a first-line approach everywhere. It's worth asking your vet about if you're dealing with large numbers of warts across the mouth.

**Interferon** (oral or injectable) has been used in cases with extensive oral papillomatosis. The evidence supporting it in dogs is not especially strong, but it's sometimes tried when warts are numerous and failing to regress.

## The Home Remedy Problem

Castor oil, apple cider vinegar, vitamin E, thuja (a homeopathic preparation): you'll find all of these recommended online for dog warts. The honest assessment is that the evidence for any of them is essentially absent. Some owners believe castor oil or vitamin E softens and shrinks warts; there's no good veterinary literature supporting this, and the more important point is that most warts they're applied to would have resolved on their own anyway.

The dangerous home remedies are the mechanical ones: tying off a wart with thread to cut off blood supply, cutting it with scissors, or using over-the-counter human wart removers. Human salicylic acid wart products are not formulated for dog skin, and applying them can cause chemical burns, significant pain, and secondary infection. Attempting to cut or ligate a wart at home risks introducing bacteria into the site and can cause serious bleeding, particularly if the growth is larger or more vascular than it looks. This is not the place for DIY.

If you're in the watchful-waiting phase and want to do something, keeping the wart clean and dry is genuinely useful. If it's in an area your dog licks or scratches, an Elizabethan collar during healing or recovery is worth using.

## Keeping an Immune System in Good Shape

Since canine papillomavirus relies on a weaker immune response to take hold, general immune health does matter here. This isn't a dramatic statement; it just means that puppies, senior dogs, and dogs on long-term immune-suppressing medication are genuinely higher risk. Keeping vaccinations current, maintaining a good diet, and managing any underlying conditions (like hypothyroidism or allergies) reduces the chance warts become a recurring problem.

Knowing your dog's age in relation to their immune maturity can be useful context. The [Dog Age Human Years calculator](/dog-age-human-years/) can help you understand where your dog is developmentally, which matters if you're dealing with a puppy whose immune system is still building.
