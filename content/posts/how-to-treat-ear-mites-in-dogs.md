Ear mites in dogs are both treatable and, with the right product, often cleared up at home. The catch that most pet owners don't realize: a single over-the-counter treatment applied once almost never does the job. You need to treat for the full mite life cycle, which runs about three weeks, and you need to check every pet in the household because ear mites spread fast between animals.

Ear mites (the species is *Otodectes cynotis*) are tiny parasitic arachnids that live in the ear canal, feeding on skin debris and ear wax. They're more common in cats than dogs, but dogs catch them readily from infected cats or other dogs. What you'll see: intense scratching at the ears, frequent head shaking, and a distinctive dark brown or black crumbly discharge that looks a lot like coffee grounds. Sometimes there's a mild odor. The ears will look dirty no matter how recently you cleaned them.

Before you treat, confirm you're actually dealing with mites and not a bacterial or yeast infection, both of which cause similar symptoms and need completely different treatment. If the discharge is yellow, greenish, or has a strongly foul smell, that's more likely an infection. If your dog's ear canal looks swollen or red and painful to the touch, don't put anything in the ear until a vet has looked at it.

## What you're actually dealing with

The life cycle is the detail that makes or breaks home treatment. Ear mites go through five stages: egg, larva, protonymph, deutonymph, and adult. The whole cycle from egg to reproducing adult takes about 18-21 days under typical conditions. Most over-the-counter treatments kill adult mites but not eggs. That's why you can apply something once, see improvement, and then watch the scratching return a week later when the eggs hatch.

Adults live for about two months. They can survive briefly in the environment, bedding, or on furniture, which means washing your dog's bedding during treatment isn't optional.

One thing that trips people up: ear mites occasionally migrate out of the ear canal and show up on the skin around the head, neck, and tail. If your [dog is scratching](/why-is-my-dog-scratching-so-much/) at areas beyond just the ears, or if you notice crusty patches around the base of the tail, that's not necessarily a separate problem.

## Confirming the diagnosis before you treat

You can often see mites yourself with a magnifying glass or a phone camera zoomed in close. Take a little of the dark discharge on a cotton ball and hold it up against a dark background in good light. Adult mites are white, tiny (barely visible), and move. If you see movement, that's a strong confirmation.

A vet confirmation is fast and inexpensive: they'll do an otoscope exam and often a quick microscope swab, which takes minutes. This is worth doing if you're not confident, if the ears look unusually inflamed, or if previous treatment didn't work. What looks like a persistent mite problem is sometimes a secondary bacterial infection that developed because the dog scratched and broke the skin inside the ear.

## Treatment options compared

There's a meaningful difference between the OTC options at the pet store and what your vet can prescribe. The table below gives you an honest comparison.

| Treatment | Type | Active Ingredient | Application | Prescription? | Typical Duration |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Revolution (selamectin) | Topical spot-on | Selamectin | Monthly, back of neck | Yes (US) | 1-2 doses |
| Bravecto Plus (cats) / Bravecto (dogs) | Topical spot-on | Fluralaner | Single dose | Yes | 1 dose often sufficient |
| Acarexx | Ear drop | Ivermectin | Into ear canal | Yes | 1-2 treatments |
| Tresaderm | Ear drop | Thiabendazole + steroids | Daily for 7-14 days | Yes | 2 weeks |
| Eradimite / Otomite Plus | OTC ear drop | Pyrethrins | Daily for 21+ days | No | 3-4 weeks |
| Nolvasan Otic | OTC ear drop | Various | Daily for 21+ days | No | 3-4 weeks |

The prescription options, particularly Revolution and single-dose Bravecto, have a significantly better track record for complete clearance. If you've already tried an OTC product for a full three weeks without improvement, that's your sign to call the vet.

**Example:** Say you have a 30-lb dog and two cats in the house. The dog gets ear mites (probably from the cats). If you treat only the dog with an OTC pyrethrin drop for 21 days, there's a good chance the cats are still carrying mites and will reinfect the dog shortly after. The more efficient approach: get a vet visit for all three animals, ask about Revolution or a similar systemic product that covers mites, and treat everyone on the same day. That single vet appointment likely prevents two or three return visits.

## How to actually clean and treat the ear at home

Don't skip the cleaning step. All that dark discharge sitting in the ear canal will reduce how well any topical treatment works, because the medication can't make proper contact with the canal walls through a layer of debris.

Here's the sequence:

1. **Soften the debris first.** Use a veterinarian-approved [ear cleaning](/ear-cleaning-dogs-guide/) solution (not water, not hydrogen peroxide, not rubbing alcohol). Zymox Otic and Virbac Epi-Otic are both commonly recommended. Squeeze a small amount into the ear canal, fold the ear flap down, and gently massage the base of the ear for about 30 seconds. You'll hear a squishing sound. That's correct.

2. **Let the dog shake.** Step back. The shaking brings loosened debris up to where you can reach it.

3. **Wipe, don't dig.** Use cotton balls or gauze squares to wipe out the outer ear canal and ear flap. Never push a cotton swab down into the ear canal. You'll compact the debris and potentially damage the eardrum.

4. **Apply the mite treatment.** Follow the package directions for your specific product. With ear drops, you're aiming for the medication to coat the inside of the canal, so apply, fold the ear down, and massage the base again briefly.

5. **Repeat.** For OTC treatments, this means daily for three to four weeks. For prescription spot-ons, follow the vet's schedule, often just once or twice total.

Wash the dog's bedding in hot water on the same day you start treatment and again at the two-week mark. Mites in the environment die off faster than mites on a host, so environmental treatment is usually not needed beyond washing bedding and vacuuming where the dog sleeps.

A proper [pet first aid kit](https://www.amazon.com/s?k=pet+first+aid+kit&tag=contentportfo-20) is worth having on hand before situations like this come up. Having cotton balls, gauze, and a syringe for ear flushing already in the house saves a lot of scrambling.

## When this is beyond home treatment

Ear mites themselves are rarely dangerous. But the scratching they cause can be. Dogs can scratch hard enough to rupture blood vessels in the ear flap, causing an aural hematoma (a painful, fluid-filled swelling of the ear flap) that requires veterinary drainage. If the ear flap looks puffy, swollen like a pillow, or feels warm and squishy, don't wait on that one.

Call a vet promptly if:
- The ear canal is visibly red, swollen, or the dog pulls away sharply when you touch the ear
- You see any bloody discharge
- Your dog is tilting its head persistently to one side (possible inner ear involvement)
- OTC treatment has been used correctly for three full weeks and the dog is still symptomatic
- The dog is a puppy under eight weeks old (some medications aren't safe for very young dogs)

The [ASPCA Poison Control Center](https://www.aspca.org/pet-care/animal-poison-control) is also worth having bookmarked. If you've used a product not labeled for dogs, or used a cat-only product on a dog (pyrethrins in high concentrations are dangerous for cats but can also cause issues in dogs if overdosed), they can help you assess the risk.

The [American Veterinary Medical Association (AVMA)](https://www.avma.org/) maintains guidance on parasites and antiparasitic medications that your vet is working from, and their resources can help you understand why your vet may recommend one product over another.

## Preventing reinfestation

Once you've cleared the mites, keep your dog on a monthly parasite prevention product that covers mites. Revolution (selamectin) is currently labeled for ear mites in cats and is frequently used off-label for dogs; your vet can advise what's appropriate for your dog's size and health status. Simparica Trio and similar broad-spectrum products cover a wide range of parasites and are worth asking about if your dog has regular contact with outdoor cats or other dogs at parks or boarding.
