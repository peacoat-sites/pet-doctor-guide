Most [pet insurance](/is-pet-insurance-worth-it-for-a-healthy-young-dog/) policies won't cover pre-existing conditions at all. That's the blunt reality, and it's why so many pet owners feel burned when they finally sign up after a diagnosis. But "most" isn't "all," and there are real options worth knowing about, some policies offer limited coverage, some conditions can be reclassified after a waiting period, and some programs are built specifically for pets with health histories.

The key is understanding exactly what insurers mean by "pre-existing," because the definition varies more than you'd expect.

A condition is generally pre-existing if it showed clinical signs, was diagnosed, or was treated before your policy's effective date. Some insurers even flag conditions that appeared during a waiting period as pre-existing. The distinction that matters most is whether the condition is **curable** or **incurable**. A curable condition (think ear infections, [urinary tract](/how-to-treat-a-cat-urinary-tract-infection-at-home/) infections, certain respiratory illnesses) can sometimes qualify for coverage after a symptom-free waiting period, usually 12 months. An incurable one (diabetes, Addison's disease, hip dysplasia already diagnosed) is typically excluded for the life of the policy, full stop.

## What "curable" vs. "incurable" actually means for your wallet

Here's where most guides gloss over the detail that matters. When an insurer calls a condition "curable," they mean it can be fully resolved with no residual disease. Ear infections, mild digestive upsets, [kennel cough](/dog-kennel-cough-treatment/) -- these generally qualify. If your dog had a single UTI two years ago and has been symptom-free since, some insurers will cover future UTIs after their lookback period clears.

Incurable conditions are another story. Allergies, epilepsy, cancer, orthopedic disease, heart disease, diabetes, these are lifetime exclusions at most companies. Some insurers also classify ligament injuries (like a torn CCL) as incurable, even when surgery is "successful," because the joint is considered permanently compromised.

A common misconception worth correcting: many people assume that switching insurers gives them a clean slate. It doesn't. Any new insurer will ask for vet records or do a medical records review, and anything documented in your pet's history is fair game for exclusion.

## The options that actually exist

The honest answer is that the market is thin. No insurer in the U.S. currently offers a standard policy that covers chronic, incurable pre-existing conditions. What does exist falls into a few real categories.

**Curable condition coverage after a symptom-free period.** Embrace Pet Insurance and ASPCA Pet Health Insurance are among the insurers that explicitly offer this pathway. Under Embrace's policy structure, curable conditions may become eligible for coverage after your pet has gone 12 months without symptoms, treatment, or medication for that condition. ASPCA Pet Health Insurance has a similar approach. The exact terms matter, so read the sample policy documents before you buy, not the marketing summary page.

**Accident-only plans.** If your pet has a chronic illness, an accident-only policy won't help with the ongoing condition but will cover new, unrelated injuries. This is a legitimate strategy for pets with, say, diabetes who are still at risk of broken bones or accidental ingestion. The premiums are lower, and coverage is narrower.

**Wellness or preventive care add-ons.** Some insurers sell wellness riders that cover routine care regardless of pre-existing status. This won't pay for managing your pet's Addison's disease, but it will cover annual exams, vaccines, and heartworm testing. It's not a substitute for illness coverage, but for some owners it offsets part of their out-of-pocket load.

**ASPCA-affiliated or nonprofit programs.** Some regional humane societies and breed-specific rescues have negotiated group insurance rates or assistance programs that may have more flexible underwriting. This varies enormously by organization and is worth asking about at adoption time.

## How to actually get the best outcome if your pet has a health history

Start with a full copy of your pet's veterinary records before you shop. You need to know what's in there, because the insurer will. Look for any diagnoses, notes about symptoms, or treatments that could trigger an exclusion. This isn't about hiding anything, it's about knowing what you're working with before you fill out an application.

When you apply, some insurers offer a process called a Medical History Review (or Pre-Enrollment Review), where they evaluate your pet's records upfront and tell you in writing what will and won't be covered. This is worth requesting. Ambiguity at enrollment leads to denied claims later. Getting the exclusions in writing before you pay the first premium is the single most protective thing you can do.

If your pet has a bilateral condition like hip dysplasia only diagnosed on one side, make sure you understand the insurer's bilateral condition policy. Many will exclude the unaffected side automatically. PetMD's veterinary resource library has plain-language explanations of common policy terms that can help you decode the fine print if the insurer's language is opaque.

**Example:** Assume a 4-year-old Labrador was treated for a single UTI 18 months ago and has had no symptoms, vet visits, or medications related to it since. Under Embrace's stated policy terms, this condition would be potentially eligible for coverage because the 12-month symptom-free window has cleared. Future UTIs would be treated as a new, coverable illness. If that same dog had been diagnosed with hypothyroidism, that condition would be excluded permanently, regardless of how well-managed it currently is. The UTI coverage is worth having; the thyroid management will stay out-of-pocket.

## If your pet has a serious chronic condition

You're probably going to be managing that condition out-of-pocket regardless of what insurer you choose. That's not a failure of the system, it's how actuarial risk works. What you can do is insure aggressively against *new* problems. A dog with well-controlled Cushing's disease can still tear a ligament, eat a sock, or develop unrelated cancer. A solid comprehensive policy that excludes the Cushing's but covers everything else still has real value.

One tool that helps: pet health savings accounts or CareCredit (which has a veterinary financing option). Neither is insurance, but both can bridge the gap when a large bill for an excluded condition arrives. The ASPCA Poison Control Center also maintains resources on medication interactions and toxic exposures that pet owners managing chronic conditions should bookmark, since polypharmacy risks are real in pets on long-term medications.

A pet first aid kit won't manage a chronic disease, but having one on hand for unrelated emergencies is a reasonable baseline.
