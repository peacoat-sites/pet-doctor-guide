Most of what you'll read about FIV cats online is either panic-inducing or suspiciously breezy. "Death sentence" on one end, "totally fine, no worries!" on the other. Neither is accurate, and both fail the cats.

FIV stands for feline immunodeficiency virus. It's a lentivirus, the same class as HIV in humans, which understandably causes concern when people first learn about it. That comparison is where the similarity ends. FIV doesn't kill cats quickly. Many FIV-positive cats live completely normal lifespans and die of something entirely unrelated. The virus matters, but it's not a countdown timer.

What it does do is gradually compromise the immune system over years, making the cat more vulnerable to secondary infections that a healthy cat would shrug off. How fast that progresses varies enormously between individuals.

## How FIV Actually Spreads

This is where a lot of well-meaning rescue volunteers get it wrong. It is common to hear that FIV cats must be the only pet in the house. That guidance persisted for years before the actual transmission data became widely understood.

FIV spreads almost entirely through deep puncture bites, the kind that happen when two cats are genuinely fighting, not the casual swat-and-retreat of a normal territorial squabble. Casual contact, shared water bowls, mutual grooming, even sleeping together, carries negligible transmission risk. The virus doesn't survive long outside the body, and saliva contact during play or grooming doesn't deliver enough viral load to infect.

The population most at risk: intact outdoor males. They fight. They bite hard and deep. That's why FIV prevalence in feral colonies runs higher than in indoor cats. In owned pets kept indoors with compatible housemates who aren't drawing blood on each other, the risk of transmission is genuinely low.

The ASPCA and the UC Davis Koret Shelter Medicine Program have both stated that FIV-positive and FIV-negative cats can be safely housed together if they're non-aggressive. That guidance has been out for years. Shelters that still automatically euthanize FIV-positive cats are operating on outdated policy, not current science.

## The Testing Rabbit Hole

Diagnosis is messier than most people realize, and this is something the standard pet-adoption pamphlet glosses straight over.

The snap test your vet runs in-clinic detects antibodies, not the virus itself. In adult cats, a positive result is meaningful. In kittens under six months old, it can be a false positive because the kitten may be carrying maternal antibodies from an FIV-positive mother but not actually be infected. Those kittens need to be retested after six months. Kittens can be euthanized or refused adoption based on snap test positives that would have cleared up on retest.

A positive snap test in an adult cat warrants a confirmatory Western blot or PCR test if there's any doubt. These cost somewhere between $60 and $150 depending on your lab and location (as of August 2026). Worth running before making any major decisions about a cat's future.

One more wrinkle: cats that received the old FIV vaccine (Fel-O-Vax FIV, discontinued but given to cats that are still alive today) will test positive on the snap test forever, even if they never had FIV. If you're adopting an older cat whose history is unknown, this is worth asking about.

## What an FIV Cat Actually Needs

The management isn't dramatically different from any other cat. The gap shows up in the details.

**Indoor living.** This is non-negotiable. Not because FIV cats will infect every cat in the neighborhood (see above), but because outdoor life means exposure to pathogens they're less equipped to fight. An upper respiratory infection that a healthy outdoor cat might shake in a week can turn into a two-week course of Clavamox and a $400 vet bill in an FIV cat with reduced immune function.

**Vet visits.** Twice yearly instead of once. That does cost money. But FIV cats can look totally fine while something is quietly brewing. Twice-yearly bloodwork catches things early, and early treatment is almost always cheaper and more effective than crisis management.

**Diet.** High-quality protein. No raw diets. This is one point worth pushing back on with the raw-feeding community: immunocompromised cats are at real risk from Salmonella and Toxoplasma in raw meat. The risk isn't theoretical. PETMD's veterinary resource library notes this specifically for FIV and FeLV cats. Stick with cooked or commercially processed food.

**Dental care.** FIV cats are prone to stomatitis, a painful and aggressive gum condition that can make eating miserable. Routine dental cleanings and even daily tooth brushing (yes, really) make a meaningful difference. **Stress reduction.** The immune system takes a hit from chronic stress, and that's amplified in a cat whose immune response is already compromised. A stable routine, vertical space, and not having to compete for resources (food bowls, litter boxes, resting spots) matters more for an FIV cat than it does for a healthy one.

## What the Timeline Usually Looks Like

| Phase | Timing | What You'll See |
|---|---|---|
| Acute infection | Weeks 1-4 post-exposure | Often nothing visible; occasional mild fever, swollen lymph nodes |
| Asymptomatic carrier phase | Months to years (often 5-10+ years) | Cat appears completely normal |
| Progressive immune decline | Variable; not all cats reach this | Recurrent infections, weight loss, dental disease, neurological signs |
| Terminal AIDS-like stage | Some cats never reach this | Severe immunosuppression, cancer, organ failure |

The asymptomatic phase is long. Most [pet owners](/raw-pet-food-recalls-are-piling-up-what-owners-need-to-know/) who adopt an FIV cat will spend the majority of that cat's life in the middle column of that table, dealing with a cat that seems totally normal. That's not denial, it's just the actual disease progression.

## Real Examples

For example, if an owner adopts a 4-year-old FIV-positive cat while already having two other cats, the situation can go smoothly.  If the vet confirms the resident cats are non-aggressive and the new cat has been neutered, cohousing can be managed safely. She followed twice-yearly vet visits, switched everyone to a high-quality wet food, and added a third [litter box](/cat-not-using-litter-box-causes/). In such a scenario, all cats can remain healthy over the following years. The FIV-positive cat's bloodwork is likely to remain stable throughout. Zero transmission events is the typical outcome when housing decisions are made thoughtfully.

Contrast that with a scenario involving a family with an FIV-positive male cat who was also intact and living with another intact male. Both were indoor-outdoor cats. The fighting was regular in this hypothetical situation. If the FIV-negative cat tested positive 18 months later, that outcome would be exactly what the transmission data predicts: intact males who fight are the primary risk population.

One more: a 7-year-old FIV cat came in last spring with a third bout of [upper respiratory infection](/cat-upper-respiratory-infection/) in six months. If the owner has been feeding a boutique raw diet and skipping annual checkups, those are likely contributing factors. Switching to a cooked high-protein food, scheduling six-month rechecks, and adding a daily lysine supplement can produce a meaningful turnaround, with zero URI episodes twelve months later.
