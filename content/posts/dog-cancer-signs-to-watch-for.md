Nearly half of all dogs over the age of ten will develop cancer. Let that sit for a second. Not "some dogs." Not "a few unlucky ones." According to the American Veterinary Medical Association, cancer is the leading cause of death in dogs older than ten, accounting for almost 50% of fatalities in that age group. I've worked in small animal clinics since 2013, and I can tell you honestly: the cases that broke my heart weren't the ones where the cancer was aggressive. They were the ones where the owners had noticed something three months earlier and talked themselves out of it.

That's what this article is really about. Not a laundry list of scary words, but the specific things that should make you pick up the phone, the things that can wait a few days, and the things that most people brush off for way too long. I've been in the room when an owner says "I thought it was just aging" about a lump that turned out to be a [mast cell tumor](/mast-cell-tumor-dogs/). That phrase haunts me a little, honestly.

The research here is genuinely sobering. The National Cancer Institute estimates that over 6 million dogs are diagnosed with cancer annually in the United States. And unlike in humans, where cancer screening is a standard part of adult care, dogs don't have routine mammograms or colonoscopies. You are the screening system. Your hands, your eyes, and your gut.

## The Signs That Actually Matter

Let me cut straight to the physical stuff, because this is where people get confused. There's a lot of noise out there about "ten warning signs" that's so general it's nearly useless. So here's what I actually pay attention to, from most to least urgent.

**A lump or bump that wasn't there before.** This one tops the list for a reason. Not every lump is cancer, obviously. Lipomas (benign fatty lumps) are incredibly common in middle-aged and older dogs, especially Labrador Retrievers. I've seen Labs come in with six or seven of them and be completely fine. But here's what most people don't realize: you absolutely cannot tell from the outside whether a lump is benign or malignant. The texture, the size, the softness, none of it tells you. A fine needle aspirate, which costs roughly $45 to $75 at most clinics, is the only way to start getting answers. Any new lump deserves that, full stop.

**Unexplained weight loss.** If your dog drops more than 10% of their body weight without a change in diet or activity, that's worth a vet call within a week. Cancer increases metabolic demand in ways that are hard to compensate for. A 50-pound dog losing five pounds in two months without explanation is not "just getting older."

**Wounds that won't heal, or sores that keep coming back in the same spot.** This one gets missed constantly. Owners treat a wound, it heals, comes back, they treat it again. Recurring ulcers or sores in the same location can signal an underlying tumor affecting blood supply or immune response.

**Sudden changes in bathroom habits or difficulty with basic functions.** Straining to urinate, blood in the urine, or difficulty swallowing can all point to mass growth in places you can't see. A reader named Janet from Phoenix emailed me last spring about her 11-year-old Beagle who had been straining to defecate for two weeks. She thought it was constipation. It was a perianal adenocarcinoma. Caught early enough for surgical intervention, which was genuinely lucky.

**Persistent coughing or labored breathing that comes on gradually.** Lung metastasis is often silent until it isn't. A dry, non-productive cough that lasts more than ten days warrants a chest X-ray conversation with your vet.

Lethargy is the big one. Dogs slow down as they age, and everyone knows it, which makes "she's just tired" the single most dangerous phrase in pet ownership. There's a difference between a dog who sleeps more at twelve than she did at three, and a dog who stopped being interested in her walk, her food, or her people. That second kind of lethargy is a symptom.

Behavioral changes, too. I've seen dogs become suddenly clingy or suddenly withdrawn before a cancer diagnosis, and in hindsight owners always say "I thought something was off, but she wasn't in pain." Dogs mask pain extraordinarily well. If your normally enthusiastic dog has gone quiet, don't just chalk it up to mood.

Bad breath that comes on suddenly and wasn't there before, especially combined with drooling or difficulty eating, can indicate oral tumors. Oral melanoma in dogs is aggressive and often caught late precisely because owners assume it's just [dental disease](/cat-dental-disease-signs/).

## What Different Cancers Actually Look Like (A Practical Comparison)

Because cancer isn't one disease, the signs vary a lot depending on type. This table isn't exhaustive, but it covers the ones I see most in practice and the ones where early detection makes the biggest difference.

| Cancer Type | Common Signs | Average Age of Onset | Early Detection Makes a Difference? |
|---|---|---|---|
| Mast cell tumor | Soft skin lump, may fluctuate in size, redness | 8-10 years | Yes, Grade I/II often curable with surgery |
| Osteosarcoma | Limb swelling, sudden severe lameness | 7-10 years (large breeds) | Somewhat, very aggressive even when caught early |
| Lymphoma | Swollen lymph nodes under jaw/behind knees, lethargy | 6-9 years | Yes, chemotherapy can extend remission 12-18 months |
| Hemangiosarcoma | Sudden collapse, pale gums, abdominal swelling | 9-11 years | Rarely, often found only in crisis |
| Oral melanoma | Mouth odor, facial swelling, difficulty eating | 10-12 years | Yes if caught before lymph node spread |
| Transitional cell carcinoma | Bloody urine, straining, recurrent UTIs | 9-11 years | Yes, prognosis much better with early surgical candidates |

The hemangiosarcoma row is the one that's kept me up at night. The spleen and heart are common sites, and there are often zero symptoms until the tumor ruptures. Golden Retrievers are disproportionately affected, with some breed-specific studies suggesting up to 1 in 5 Goldens develop it. Pale gums and sudden collapse are an emergency. If your dog collapses and their gums look white or gray instead of pink, you're going to an emergency clinic [right now](/h5n1-in-cats-what-every-owner-must-know-right-now/), not calling to ask questions.

## How to Do a Monthly Home Check

I started recommending this to owners about six years ago and I genuinely believe it catches things earlier. Takes about five minutes. Run your hands slowly over your dog's entire body, from nose to tail, including armpits, groin, and behind the ears. You're feeling for anything that wasn't there last month: a lump, a thickened patch of skin, an area that makes them flinch when you touch it.

The flinching part matters. Dogs won't cry out the way we expect. A subtle pull away from your hand, a muscle twitch, or even a lick-lick-lick at one spot can indicate localized pain.

Keep a simple note on your phone. "Monthly check done, no new lumps, left shoulder lipoma unchanged." When you go to the vet and they ask "how long has that been there?", you'll actually know.

One thing only someone who has done this repeatedly would know: the armpit and the groin are where people consistently forget to check. Those areas are warm and a little awkward to access, and they're also where lymph nodes can become enlarged early.

As of August 2026, there are also at-home cancer screening blood tests marketed directly to owners, like the Nu.Q Vet Cancer Test. The science behind liquid biopsy in dogs is promising but genuinely still evolving. The AVMA hasn't issued formal guidance endorsing these as replacements for diagnostic imaging or pathology. I'd treat them as a potential early flag that then leads you to an actual vet workup, not a ruling-in or ruling-out tool. PetMD's veterinary resource library has a decent breakdown of where the evidence currently stands if you want to read into it more.

## When to Go Today vs. When to Wait Until Monday

This is the practical part I know you came for. I'm going to be direct.

**Go today (or to an emergency clinic if it's after hours):**
- Sudden collapse or extreme weakness
- Pale, white, or grayish gums
- Abdomen that looks suddenly bloated or painful to the touch
- Labored breathing that came on fast
- Seizures with no prior history

**Call your regular vet first thing tomorrow:**
- A new lump you've just discovered
- Bloody urine without other acute symptoms
- Persistent cough lasting more than one to two weeks
- Unexplained weight loss over a few weeks

**Schedule a routine appointment within two weeks:**
- A lump that's been there a while but you've never had checked
- Gradual change in appetite or energy
- Bad breath with drooling or difficulty eating

The distinction between "today" and "two weeks" isn't about how scared you are, it's about the physiological urgency of the symptom. A lump that's been stable for six months isn't likely to be an emergency tonight. A dog with white gums who just collapsed absolutely is.
