That's the dose at which xylitol can trigger life-threatening hypoglycemia in a dog, according to the ASPCA Animal Poison Control Center, and it's a smaller amount than most people can visualize. For a 20-pound Beagle, we're talking about roughly 0.9 grams of xylitol, which you'd find in as few as two or three pieces of sugar-free gum.

## Why Xylitol Is So Dangerous (And It's Not the Reason You Think)

Most people assume a toxin causes problems because it's a foreign chemical the body can't process. With xylitol, it's weirder than that. In dogs, xylitol triggers a massive, inappropriate release of insulin. The dog's pancreas interprets xylitol as a glucose spike and floods the bloodstream with insulin, which crashes blood sugar fast. We're talking about drops to 30 or 40 mg/dL (normal is roughly 70-120 mg/dL) within 30 to 60 minutes in some cases.

At higher doses, the mechanism shifts. The current understanding from veterinary toxicology is that doses at or above 0.5 g/kg can cause hepatic necrosis, basically the liver begins dying. The [ASPCA Animal Poison Control Center](https://www.aspca.org/pet-care/animal-poison-control) has documented this dose-response relationship extensively, and it's part of why xylitol sits in a different category than many common pet toxins. A small exposure is a hypoglycemia emergency. A larger exposure is a multi-organ emergency.

Why are dogs uniquely sensitive? Humans, cats, and most other mammals don't release insulin in response to xylitol the way dogs do. Ferrets appear similarly reactive, worth knowing if you have both animals. But for our purposes, this is a dog problem, and a serious one.

This is where I want to slow down, because the public conversation about xylitol is still largely stuck on "sugar-free gum," and that framing is dangerously outdated.

The shift happened gradually over the last several years as xylitol became a popular sugar substitute marketed as beneficial for [dental health](/small-breed-dental-health-dogs/). As of 2026, you'll find it in:

**Sugar-free gum**: Still the most concentrated source. Some brands pack 0.3 to 0.4 grams of xylitol per piece. A standard pack of 15 pieces could contain enough to hospitalize a large dog.

**Peanut butter**: This one catches owners off guard constantly. Several brands, including Nuts 'N More and P28, used xylitol as of the last few years. Always check the label. If it says "sugar alcohol" or "xylitol" anywhere, don't use it for pill pockets or training treats.

**Vitamins and supplements**: Gummy vitamins for kids, sugar-free fiber gummies, some probiotic chews marketed for humans. These often sit at dog-nose height on a coffee table.

**Oral care products**: Mouthwash, toothpaste (human formulations), some dental rinses.

**Baked goods, condiments, and "keto" products**: The low-carb and keto market has dramatically expanded xylitol's reach. Baked goods, syrups, and even some marinades made for people on low-sugar diets may use it.

I had a client a few years ago whose dog got into a bag of sugar-free baking mix she'd ordered online. She checked the front label for "artificial sweeteners" and saw nothing. Xylitol was listed sixth in the ingredients. The dog survived, but it was a 36-hour hospital stay and a genuinely frightening situation.

|---|---|---|---|
| Under 50 mg/kg | May cause mild GI upset only | Variable | Call ASPCA Poison Control |
| 50-100 mg/kg (0.05-0.1 g/kg) | Hypoglycemia risk begins | 30-60 minutes | Emergency vet immediately |

One thing that gets confusing: the gum or product label won't tell you the xylitol dose per piece in milligrams most of the time. You'll need to check total xylitol in the package and divide by piece count, or call ASPCA Poison Control and let them do the math. That's genuinely what they're there for.

## What to Do If Your Dog Ate Xylitol

First, stop Googling and call. The ASPCA Animal Poison Control Center is (888) 426-4435, and there's a $95 consultation fee as of mid-2026 (worth every cent, they walk you through the exact risk calculation for your dog's weight and what was consumed). Your emergency vet is the other option, and if you can go in person, do that.

Do not induce vomiting at home unless Poison Control specifically tells you to. I know that's counterintuitive. The problem is that if your dog is already hypoglycemic, vomiting could make things worse. This is one of those cases where the home remedy can genuinely cause harm.

While you're on the phone, have the product packaging in front of you. They'll ask you for the product name, how much xylitol is in it (listed in ingredients or a nutrition label), and your dog's weight.

If your dog is already showing symptoms: wobbly walking, weakness, vomiting, tremors, or loss of consciousness, skip the phone call and get to an emergency vet right now. Those symptoms mean absorption has already happened.

Here's a real scenario I've seen play out:

**45-pound Labrador mix ate 8 pieces of Trident sugar-free gum** (approximately 2.4 grams of xylitol total, or roughly 0.23 g/kg) → Owner called ASPCA Poison Control within 20 minutes, drove to emergency vet → Dog received IV dextrose for 18 hours, blood glucose monitoring every two hours, was discharged in stable condition after 24 hours. Total vet bill: approximately $1,400. Dog was fine.

**10-pound Chihuahua mix got into a bag of xylitol-sweetened cookies** (estimated 4+ grams consumed, over 0.8 g/kg) → Owner noticed lethargy four hours later, waited until morning thinking the dog was "just tired" → Dog presented in acute liver failure → Despite aggressive treatment, outcome was poor.

I'm not sharing that second one to scare you. I'm sharing it because the waiting is the part that kills. If there's any chance your dog ate xylitol, the window for intervention is measured in minutes to hours, not days.

## Keeping Your House Safe

Honestly, the best approach is treating xylitol the same way you'd treat your prescription medications: locked or stored high, and never assumed to be out of reach. Dogs are remarkably good at getting into things on coffee tables, low shelves, bags left on the floor, and purses.

A few habits that help: read ingredient labels on anything you use as a dog treat or pill pocket, especially nut butters. Keep your sugar-free gum in a zipped bag inside a closed bag. If you take gummy vitamins, keep them in a cabinet, not on the counter.

The [American Veterinary Medical Association (AVMA)](https://www.avma.org/) has a pet poison prevention resource that lists common hazards, and it's a reasonable starting point for a general household audit if you want to go further.
