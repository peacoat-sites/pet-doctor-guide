A single sneeze means almost nothing. A cat who sneezes repeatedly over several days, or who has discharge coming from the eyes or nose alongside it, is telling you something worth paying attention to. The good news: most causes of cat sneezing are benign and self-limiting. The less good news: a handful of causes are serious enough that waiting too long makes treatment harder.

Here's how to tell the difference.

## The Most Likely Culprit: Upper Respiratory Infections

Feline URIs account for the overwhelming majority of cat sneezing, especially in cats who've spent any time around other cats, shelters, catteries, multi-cat households. The two biggest offenders are feline herpesvirus type 1 (FHV-1) and feline calicivirus (FCV). Between them, they cause the vast majority of feline [upper respiratory](/cat-upper-respiratory-infection/) disease.

FHV-1 is particularly stubborn. Once a cat is infected, the virus stays dormant in nerve tissue for life. Stress, illness, or steroid use can reactivate it, which is why some cats sneeze for a week every time the household routine changes. This isn't a failure of treatment, it's just how the virus works. The [American Veterinary Medical Association (AVMA)](https://www.avma.org/) recognizes FHV-1 as one of the core pathogens covered by routine feline vaccination, and while the vaccine doesn't prevent infection entirely, it typically reduces the severity of signs.

Bacterial infections, most commonly *Bordetella bronchiseptica* or *Mycoplasma* species, often layer on top of viral URIs rather than causing sneezing on their own. If you see thick yellow-green discharge, that bacterial component is a real possibility, and that's usually when antibiotics become appropriate. Clear watery discharge, on the other hand, often points to a primary viral infection or an irritant. Antibiotics won't help with either of those.

## Household Irritants: The Overlooked Cause

Cats have a far more sensitive sense of smell than humans, and products that smell mild to us can be genuinely noxious to them. Products that smell mild to us can be genuinely noxious to them. Plug-in air fresheners, scented candles, aerosol cleaning sprays, cigarette smoke, and even some essential oil diffusers can trigger sneezing that looks exactly like an infection.

The diagnostic test here is free and takes about a week: remove the suspected irritant from the room where your cat spends most of its time and watch what happens. If sneezing stops or significantly improves within a few days, you have your answer. No [vet visit](/cat-annual-vet-visit-what-to-expect/) needed.

A less obvious irritant source: dusty cat litter. Some clumping litters produce a fine silica dust that some cats react to strongly. Switching to a low-dust formula (unscented, fine-grain) is a simple change worth trying before assuming the cause is infectious.

## Dental Disease and Nasal Polyps

This one surprises most owners: tooth root infections in the upper jaw, particularly the upper carnassial teeth (the large cheek teeth), sit directly below the nasal cavity. An abscess there can cause unilateral nasal discharge and sneezing that doesn't respond to anything because nobody's treating the actual problem.

If your cat's sneezing is accompanied by facial swelling, pawing at the mouth, or drooling, [dental disease](/cat-dental-disease-signs/) belongs at the top of your list. A vet can often identify the problem on physical exam, though a dental X-ray confirms it.

Nasal polyps are fleshy benign growths that develop in the nasal passages or nasopharynx. They're more common in young cats and in cats who've had recurrent URIs. The sneezing they cause tends to be noisy, often accompanied by stertor (that low snoring or congested sound), and it doesn't respond to antibiotics or antivirals because it's mechanical, not infectious. Diagnosis requires rhinoscopy or imaging.

## When to Go to the Vet: A Decision Framework

Not every sneezing cat needs a same-day appointment. Here's how to triage it honestly.

| Situation | Urgency | Why |
|---|---|---|
| Occasional sneezing, no discharge, eating normally | Monitor at home 5-7 days | Very likely minor irritant or mild self-limiting URI |
| Clear watery discharge from nose only, still eating | Schedule within 2-3 days | Possible early URI; watch for progression |
| Yellow/green discharge from nose or eyes | Vet within 24-48 hours | Likely bacterial involvement; antibiotics often needed |
| Not eating, lethargic, mouth breathing | Same-day or emergency | Respiratory compromise; can deteriorate quickly |
| Sneezing blood, even once | Vet within 24 hours | Epistaxis has a wide differential: foreign body, polyp, hypertension, fungal infection, or mass |
| Chronic sneezing (3+ weeks) with no clear cause | Scheduled workup | Requires rhinoscopy, imaging, and possibly biopsy |
| Kitten under 8 weeks old with any of the above | Same-day | Very young kittens decompensate fast |

Young kittens and senior cats deserve a lower threshold for a vet visit than healthy adults. A 10-week-old kitten with a URI can go downhill in 24 hours.

## Chronic Sneezing: What a Workup Actually Looks Like

If a cat has been sneezing on and off for more than three weeks and standard treatment hasn't resolved it, the conversation changes. This is where "rhinitis of undetermined origin" (sometimes called lymphoplasmacytic rhinitis) enters the picture, an inflammatory condition that may or may not have an identifiable infectious trigger.

A thorough workup for chronic sneezing in cats typically includes:

- Full blood panel and urinalysis (screens for systemic disease, checks organ function before anesthesia)
- Thoracic and skull radiographs
- Rhinoscopy under general anesthesia, allowing direct visualization of the nasal passages and collection of biopsies or flush samples for culture
- Fungal testing for *Cryptococcus neoformans*, which is endemic in various parts of North America and causes granulomatous nasal disease

[AAHA-accredited hospitals](https://www.aaha.org/) will typically have rhinoscopy equipment in-house or a referral relationship with an internal medicine specialist who does. If your vet proposes rhinoscopy and you're wondering if it's excessive, it usually isn't for a cat who's been sneezing for months. Getting a tissue diagnosis changes the treatment entirely.

## At-Home Support for Mild URIs

For a cat with a mild URI who's eating, alert, and not showing eye involvement or colored discharge, there's reasonable supportive care you can provide at home while you monitor.

Keep the nasal passages clear: a soft damp cloth to gently wipe discharge from the nose, twice a day if needed. A humidifier in the room can help loosen congestion, the goal is around 50% relative humidity, not a steam room. Never use Vicks, eucalyptus, or menthol products near cats. These are toxic.

Appetite often drops with URIs because cats depend heavily on smell to find food appetizing. Warming wet food slightly (body temperature, not hot) releases more aroma and can encourage a reluctant eater. If a cat hasn't eaten in more than 24-36 hours, that's a vet call regardless of what else is going on.
