It started when a dog owner in Hawaii bought what looked like a perfectly normal can of Pedigree off a third-party seller's shelf. The label was right. The can looked sealed. There was no obvious reason for alarm. What she didn't know was that those cans had already been condemned by the manufacturer, flagged as failures during quality control, and sent to a destruction vendor to be disposed of. Somewhere between the factory and the shredder, someone intercepted them and put them back into the retail stream.

That's not a contamination story. That's fraud. And it changes what you need to watch for as a pet owner.

On July 2, 2026, Mars Petcare US issued a voluntary recall of two lots of PEDIGREE Can [High Protein](/best-high-protein-dog-food-for-weight-loss/) Chopped Chicken & Duck Flavor 13.2 oz, lot codes 613C3KKCFC and 613C1KKCFC. According to the FDA recall announcement, these cans may contain sharp metal and plastic fragments that could cause choking, GI lacerations, or intestinal blockages. The part that makes this recall different from every other one this year: the product never should have been available to buy. Ever. Mars had already rejected it internally before it cleared the company's own quality standards. The food was supposed to be destroyed.

## Why This Recall Is Different From the Other Nine

Federal regulators have logged at least 10 pet food recall events in 2026 already. The range has been wide: Salmonella contamination tied to Albright's Raw and Elite Treats, a [thiamine deficiency](/thiamine-deficiency-in-cats-what-the-quest-food-recall-reveals/) problem with Go Raw LLC and Steve's Real Food, elevated mineral levels in a Hill's product, and now this. Each of those earlier recalls happened because something went wrong during production or formulation. A pathogen got through. A nutrient calculation was off. Those are painful failures, but they're failures within a system that was at least trying to get the product right.

The Pedigree situation is categorically different. Mars confirmed the diverted cans had already failed internal quality control. They were never approved for sale. The company sent them to a third-party destruction vendor, and somewhere in that chain, the product was intercepted and sold anyway. Mars is working with law enforcement to figure out exactly how it happened. As of the recall announcement, no pet illnesses or injuries had been reported, which is fortunate, but the physical hazard, sharp metal and plastic fragments inside sealed cans, is real and not visible without opening the food.

What most people don't realize is that a condemned product re-entering the supply chain is not something a normal quality check catches on the consumer end. You're not going to see a damaged seal. The label won't look wrong. You have no way of knowing the can you're looking at was supposed to be in a landfill two months ago.

Here's the part Hawaii's Department of Health flagged specifically in their July 7, 2026 public alert: because this product was sold by an unauthorized third party, there is no distribution data. None. That means regulators cannot tell you which states received the cans, which stores stocked them, or even how many units made it out. With a normal recall, you get a retailer list. You can call your store. You can check whether your ZIP code was affected.

With this one, you can't do that. The usual recall playbook doesn't apply.

That absence of distribution data is genuinely unusual. It's also the argument for checking lot codes yourself rather than waiting for a store or retailer to notify you. The two affected lot codes are printed on the can bottom: 613C3KKCFC and 613C1KKCFC. If you have cans of this product, that's the first thing to check.

## Where This Food Was Likely Being Sold

The fraud aspect points pretty clearly toward third-party and discount marketplaces. Rejected product that bypasses a manufacturer's destruction process doesn't typically end up on the shelves of large national retailers with tight inventory controls. It ends up on Amazon third-party listings, discount liquidation stores, Facebook Marketplace, and smaller online pet supply shops where sourcing verification is minimal or nonexistent.

I've seen this pattern before with human food products too, condemned or near-expiry stock getting offloaded through gray-market channels at prices that look like a deal. If you're buying [pet food](/raw-pet-food-recalls-are-piling-up-what-owners-need-to-know/) from a third-party Amazon seller, a liquidation site, or any source that isn't an authorized retailer for that brand, the price discount is not necessarily the good news it appears to be.

## What to Actually Do Right Now

Check your pantry first. If you have any Pedigree Can High Protein Chopped Chicken & Duck Flavor in 13.2 oz cans, look at the lot code stamped on the bottom. If it reads 613C3KKCFC or 613C1KKCFC, stop feeding it immediately. Do not open more cans from the same purchase. Mars's recall guidance says to contact them directly for a refund or replacement, even if you bought through a channel that isn't part of their normal authorized retail network.

If your dog has already eaten food from one of these lots, watch for signs of GI distress: vomiting, refusing to eat, straining, blood in stool, or obvious discomfort in the abdomen. Those symptoms after eating a recalled product with potential sharp fragments are not "wait and see" symptoms. That's an emergency vet call. Most cases won't involve injury, especially if only a small amount was consumed, but GI lacerations from metal fragments are serious and can deteriorate fast.

Going forward, the single most practical habit you can build is registering your pet food products with the FDA's recall notification system, or bookmarking the AVMA's recalls and safety alerts page, which is updated regularly and covers species-specific recalls. The Cooldown reported in late July 2026 that this step is something veterinary professionals have been pushing specifically because 2026's recall volume has been high enough that waiting to hear about it through social media means you're often days behind.

Buy from authorized retailers when you can. Be skeptical of prices that seem significantly below market on name-brand wet food. And if a deal looks too good, especially on a premium or specialized product from a third-party seller with limited reviews, trust that instinct.

The Mars recall is being handled through law enforcement now, which is the right call. But the broader lesson is that your dog's food supply doesn't start at the store shelf. It starts a lot earlier than that, and sometimes what's supposed to leave the system doesn't.
