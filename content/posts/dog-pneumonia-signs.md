Pneumonia in dogs can turn life-threatening within 48 hours. If your dog is breathing with visible effort, has a fever over 104°F, or seems weak and won't eat, don't wait for a Monday appointment. Get to a vet or emergency clinic now. For dogs who are coughing but eating normally and acting fairly alert, you have a bit more time, though still measured in hours, not days.

That said, recognizing pneumonia early is genuinely hard. The early signs overlap with simple kennel cough, allergies, and even [heart disease](/a-cheaper-generic-for-dogs-with-heart-disease-is-now-available/). Here's how to tell the difference, what to watch at home, and exactly when to escalate.

## What Pneumonia Actually Does in a Dog's Body

Pneumonia is an infection or inflammation of the lung tissue itself, not just the airways. That distinction matters because it's why a coughing dog with pneumonia is in worse shape than a coughing dog with bronchitis. When the air sacs (alveoli) fill with fluid, pus, or inflammatory material, oxygen transfer drops. The dog works harder to breathe, gets less oxygen anyway, and can deteriorate rapidly.

There are two main types you'll encounter: infectious pneumonia, usually bacterial (sometimes following a viral illness like distemper or influenza), and aspiration pneumonia, caused when a dog inhales vomit, regurgitated food, or liquid medications. Aspiration pneumonia is particularly nasty and fast. If your dog vomited and then started coughing or breathing oddly within a few hours, treat that as an emergency regardless of how "fine" they seem otherwise.

Fungal pneumonia exists too (Blastomyces, Histoplasma, and others depending on your region), but it's less common and tends to develop more slowly. Your vet's geographic location and your dog's travel history factor into that workup.

## Signs That Mean Go Now vs. Signs That Mean Call Today

Not every symptom carries the same urgency. The table below is based on standard triage logic used in veterinary emergency medicine:

| Sign | Urgency | Why |
|---|---|---|
| Visible effort to breathe (belly heaving, neck extended, elbows out) | Emergency, go immediately | Indicates severe oxygen compromise |
| Blue or gray gums | Emergency, go immediately | Active oxygen deprivation |
| Fever over 104°F with cough | Same day, within hours | Serious systemic infection |
| Rapid breathing at rest (over ~40 breaths/min in a resting adult dog) | Same day, within hours | Respiratory compromise building |
| Wet, productive cough with lethargy | Today, don't wait until tomorrow | Early or progressing pneumonia |
| Decreased appetite + persistent cough lasting 48+ hours | Call today, appointment within 24 hrs | Could be early pneumonia or worsening URI |
| Mild cough, eating and drinking normally, no fever | Monitored at home 24 hrs, then call if no improvement | May be kennel cough or mild URI |

Counting breaths is something any owner can do. Watch your dog's chest for 15 seconds while they're resting (not sleeping deeply). Multiply by four. A healthy resting adult dog breathes 15-30 times per minute. Anything consistently above 40 at rest warrants a call to your vet immediately.

## The Symptoms Owners Miss

The obvious ones are coughing and labored breathing. But dogs with pneumonia also frequently show:

- **Nasal discharge** that's yellow-green or rusty-brown rather than clear. Clear discharge is usually less alarming. Colored discharge suggests infection.
- **Sudden exercise intolerance.** A dog who normally romps at the dog park and now walks to the end of the driveway and wants to turn back is telling you something is wrong with how much oxygen they can move.
- **Preference for lying in a strange position.** Dogs with respiratory distress often sleep sitting upright or with their front end propped up. It's uncomfortable to lie flat when your lungs are compromised.
- **Crackling or wheezing sounds** audible without a stethoscope. If you can hear your dog's breathing across the room, that's not normal.
- **Fever without obvious cause.** Rectal temperature is the only reliable way to check. A normal dog runs about 101-102.5°F. Anything above 103.5°F deserves attention. Above 104°F, see a vet today.

One thing to watch for and not dismiss: a brief apparent "improvement" in a dog with pneumonia. Sometimes dogs seem slightly perkier for a few hours, then crash. This isn't a reason to cancel the appointment you already made.

## What the Vet Will Actually Do

Diagnosis requires chest X-rays. Bloodwork (complete blood count and chemistry panel) shows the degree of infection and helps assess organ function. Your vet may also take a tracheal wash or bronchoalveolar lavage to identify the specific bacteria causing the infection, which matters because broad-spectrum antibiotics don't always hit the right target.

Treatment for bacterial pneumonia involves antibiotics (oral if the dog is stable enough to go home, IV if hospitalized), sometimes nebulization therapy to loosen secretions, coupage (percussion of the chest wall to help move mucus), and supportive care like fluids and anti-nausea medications if needed. Severely affected dogs need oxygen supplementation. Some will need ICU-level care.

Do not expect a one-week antibiotic course to fully resolve this. Treatment for bacterial pneumonia in dogs commonly runs 3-6 weeks, and repeat chest X-rays are needed to confirm the lungs have actually cleared. Stopping antibiotics early because the dog "seems fine" is one of the most common reasons pneumonia relapses.

PetMD's veterinary resource library has solid overviews of pneumonia types and treatment approaches if you want background reading before your vet appointment.

## Helping Your Dog Breathe More Comfortably at Home (While You Wait)

There's limited safe intervention you can do at home, and that's honest, not a cop-out. If your vet has diagnosed mild pneumonia and your dog is being treated outpatient, here's what actually helps between appointments:

Keep the environment calm and cool. Heat increases respiratory demand. Don't take them on walks or let them run while actively sick. Encourage rest.

A humidifier in the room can help loosen secretions (cool mist, not hot steam near a dog who might burn themselves or get too warm). A short stay in a steamy bathroom before prescribed nebulization isn't harmful but isn't a treatment either.

If your dog is on antibiotics and you're giving them pills wrapped in food, make sure they're eating them completely. Partial dosing is worse than no dosing in terms of bacterial resistance development.

If your dog's condition worsens at any point, including at 2 a.m., call an [emergency vet](/when-to-take-your-dog-to-the-emergency-vet/). ASPCA Poison Control won't help here, but many emergency clinics have triage nurses who can tell you over the phone whether to come in immediately.
